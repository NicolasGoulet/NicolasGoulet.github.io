### Prologue

Now, the prologue. I still am amazed by the freshness of its wit, although I am reading a recent translation into french. It reminds me of the famous prologue of *Mademoiselle de Maupin*, a most delicious book by Théophile de Gautier. The two very first words *Lecteur oisif* (otiose reader in English), serve as a most powerful attention grabber : can't we help but smile in the face of this accusation? For my part, I will refer to great Borges, who argued for the act of reading being more challenging act than writing. Is the act of reading Don Quichottes otiose? As meaning, serving no purpose? It cannot be, and this prologue proposes to serve as an answer to this question. And before moving on, this way of adressing the reader in the beggining of the work brings to mind the accusations of Beaudelaire in *Les Fleurs du Mal*... Hypocritical reader!

### Religious and Racial Tensions : Impacts on Don Quichottes

This topic is the subject of more than one PhD dissertation: let's tread lightly. After the end of the Reconquista, the Iberian Peninsula remained deeply shaped by centuries of Muslim rule and coexistence among communities. This influence is still felt today, whether through the sheer quantity of Spanish and Portuguese words with Arabic roots or through surviving buildings shaped by Islamic architectural traditions.

“Old Christian” did not simply mean a Christian whose family had remained Christian under Muslim rule. In early modern Spain, it described someone who claimed an ancestry free of Jewish or Muslim forebears, in contrast with “New Christians” or *conversos*. Under the discriminatory statutes of *limpieza de sangre*—purity of blood—being able to certify that status became a *hot commodity*.

### First Adventure

Very rapidly, it is established that Don Quichotte's actions are not all that chivalrous, whether we consider their consequences or the actions themselves. One thing that quite surprised me is the actual violence. Our knight gravely injures not one but two innocent muleteers who approach the weapons he has left by a watering trough. He warns the first, then strikes him so badly that the man is left stunned on the ground. When a second muleteer arrives, Don Quichotte gives him no warning before striking him and splitting his scalp “into four.” It is easy to misread this line as making four heads where there was one, but the text does not say that either man dies. The prudest among us might deem such attacks unwarranted or even unseemly. This would be an error in judgment, as Señor Quijana is merely performing an act most befitting a *knight errant*: the vigil of arms. This ritual involves keeping watch over one's weapons on the eve of being knighted, demonstrating bravery and piety; anyone disrupting it would call his value as a knight into question.

This first adventure, of course, ends up extremely poorly for our knight. Without spoiling anything, he ends up injured back at his village, both in body and spirit, as he is still convinced that he must maintain his chivalrous ways, helping the weak and necessitous, wherever they might be. ## Start of the Second Part

This blog entry will end with a look at what the 1605 volume itself calls its Second Part—not to be confused with Cervantes's separate 1615 sequel. I imagine the start of this internal Second Part is around when people realize *Don Quichotte* might not only be fun and giggles; it can get quite complex. Rapidly, the reader realizes the tone has shifted and that the narrative has become drier and more convoluted, at least until we return to the adventures of our knight. We have an anonymous narrator talking to us about events we have yet to see, then claiming that the rest of the story comes from an Arabic manuscript by Cide Hamete Benengeli. The narrator finds a Morisco passerby who can read Arabic and hires him to translate it into Castilian. We therefore receive the story through several voices and possible biases rather than through a simple transcription by a random passerby.
